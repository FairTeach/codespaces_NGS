# Guide to Preparing a Short Omics Project Proposal

Use this guide alongside [`Omics_Project_Proposal_short.md`](./Omics_Project_Proposal_short.md). This format retains the scientific reasoning and project design of a proposal while removing grant-specific sections that are not essential to the assignment.

## 1. Select and connect the articles

1. Choose **two or three review articles** from the [article selection list](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1mZdAAMBhLZ1a-GPtAdsS6eO52a1VSLMeDBJUzhkNeM4/edit?usp=sharing).
2. Identify a common theme, complementary technologies, or a gap that links the selected articles.
3. Use relevant primary research papers where needed to support the proposed study design, methods, or interpretation.
4. Narrow the topic to one research question that can be answered by a feasible study.

Before drafting, complete this sentence: **“We want to find out whether/how [specific factor or process] affects [specific outcome] in [defined context], using [omics approach].”**

## 2. Build a clear scientific argument

### Title and Summary

Write the summary after drafting the proposal. In 200–250 words, state the problem, knowledge gap, research question or hypothesis, objectives, main methods, and expected contribution.

### Introduction and Background

Move from the general problem to the specific gap:

- Why does the problem matter biologically, clinically, or technologically?
- What do the selected reviews tell us, and how do their topics or approaches relate?
- What remains unknown or unresolved?
- Why is the proposed study a useful way to address that gap?

Synthesize the articles into a coherent rationale rather than writing separate summaries of each paper.

### Research Question, Hypothesis, and Objectives

State one focused research question and a testable hypothesis. Define **2–3 specific objectives** that follow from the knowledge gap. Each objective should be addressed by the methods and linked to an expected result.

### Materials and Methods — the core section

Explain how the study would answer the question and meet its objectives:

1. **Study design:** What will be measured, compared, or followed, and why is this design appropriate?
2. **Materials or data:** What samples, model system, or public dataset will be used? Define the relevant groups and a plausible source.
3. **Omics approach:** Which technology or data type will be used, what will it measure, and why is it suitable? Explain the role of each omics layer if using more than one.
4. **Experimental or computational workflow:** Describe the main steps from sample/data selection through quality control and analysis to biological interpretation.
5. **Analysis plan:** Identify the main comparisons or analytical strategies and explain how they address the objectives. Detailed software inventories are unnecessary.
6. **Controls and validation:** Explain how data quality will be assessed and how important findings could be validated or checked independently.
7. **Feasibility and responsible research:** Briefly address access to materials/data, reproducibility, limitations, and any relevant ethics or privacy issues.

The methods should receive the most space in the report. Choose a manageable design and justify its key decisions; do not add technologies simply to make the proposal appear more complex.

### Expected Results and Interpretation

Describe the types of results the study might produce, how they would address each objective, and how they could be interpreted. Include a plausible alternative result and its implications. These are predictions, not findings: do not invent data or write expected outcomes as though the work has already been done.

### Significance, Innovation, and Limitations

Explain what is novel or valuable in the proposed combination of question, data, and methods. State the potential scientific or translational contribution without overclaiming clinical impact. Identify the main limitations and, briefly, how they might shape interpretation or a next step.

### Conclusions

Close with the proposal’s central contribution and the most important next step.

## 3. Plan figures and tables

Include **4–5 relevant figures or tables**, including at least one original methods workflow. A workflow could show:

**Samples or dataset → omics measurement/data selection → quality control → analysis → validation → biological interpretation**

Adapt the steps to the project. Label the inputs, main methods, and expected outputs. Figures should clarify the written proposal rather than introduce work not described in the methods. Add captions, cite sources, and credit any adapted material.

## 4. Keep the scope appropriate

- Focus on one research question and 2–3 linked objectives.
- Use two or three selected review articles to build the rationale; add primary literature selectively.
- Prefer a clearly defined, realistic sample/model plan or a suitable public dataset.
- Use only the omics approaches needed to answer the question.
- Explain the core design, analysis, controls, and validation without turning the methods into a protocol manual.
- Do not include funding strategies, budgets, detailed work packages, Gantt charts, team biographies, or extensive risk matrices.
- Check that the summary, background, objectives, methods, expected results, and conclusions all describe the same study.

## 5. Final check

- [ ] Two or three selected review articles are connected to the research question.
- [ ] The research question, hypothesis, and objectives are specific and aligned.
- [ ] Materials and Methods is the central and most developed section.
- [ ] Samples/data, omics approach, analysis, controls, validation, and feasibility are clear.
- [ ] Expected results are predictions and include a plausible alternative interpretation.
- [ ] Significance, limitations, and relevant ethical or privacy issues are addressed.
- [ ] The 4–5 figures/tables are relevant, captioned, and cited; at least one is an original workflow.
- [ ] The report is 12–15 pages excluding references and citations are consistent.
- [ ] Team contributions are stated, and the presentation lasts 12–15 minutes.
